Ask how high a place is and you are asking how far it stands above a sea that is not there. Height above sea level shapes almost everything: where rain falls and snow lies, what grows, how hard it is to breathe, where rivers run and where people have chosen to build. Yet most maps flatten it into a few brown smudges. So I wanted one map of the whole planet's height that stays sharp whether you are looking at a continent or a single valley, and a set of plain answers to plain questions. How much of the land is really mountainous? Which countries stand highest? How high do people actually live?

The map below is drawn in your browser from the open terrain tiles first built by Mapzen and now hosted by Amazon, which stitch together the space shuttle's SRTM survey, the global GMTED2010 and ETOPO1 models and national surveys from the United States to New Zealand. Every pixel is a height in metres. The page turns those heights into colour and shading as you go, so you can zoom from the Tibetan Plateau to a hill in your own county, and click anywhere to read its height. For the numbers I used the same tiles at their coarsest level, a world grid of {{grid.px|int}} by {{grid.px|int}} cells, about {{grid.km_equator|f1}} km across at the equator, with every cell weighted by its true area.

02 · The shape of the landMost of the world is low

Line up every square kilometre of land from the highest to the lowest and you get a hypsometric curve, the oldest and simplest picture of a planet's relief. It is surprisingly flat. The average height of the world's land is {{land.mean|int}} m, but half of it lies below {{land.median|int}} m. Only {{land.above.1000|pct}} is more than 1,000 m up, {{land.above.2000|pct}} more than 2,000 m, {{land.above.3000|pct}} more than 3,000 m and {{land.above.5000|pct1}} more than 5,000 m.

How much of the world's land is above each height

Share of land area higher than each height, from the zoom 4 terrain grid weighted by true cell area. Lakes are left out; ice sheets count at their surface. Hover for values.

Antarctica changes the picture more than any mountain range. Its ice sheet is a dome {{antarctica.mean|int}} m high on average over {{antarctica.area_m|f1}} million km², so {{antarctica.above.2000|pct}} of the continent stands above 2,000 m. Take it away and the rest of the world's land averages {{noant.mean|int}} m, with {{noant.above.2000|pct1}} above 2,000 m and {{noant.above.1000|pct}} above 1,000 m. Take away Greenland's ice too and the average drops to {{noice.mean|int}} m.

Seen against the whole Earth, land is a thin crust on a mostly drowned planet: {{earth.above0|pct}} of the surface stands above sea level, and the sea floor lies on average {{earth.sea_depth|int}} m down, more than four times deeper than the land is high.

03 · Continents and countriesFrom Tajikistan to the Netherlands

By continent, Antarctica is the highest by far ({{continents.0.mean|int}} m on average, all of it ice), then Asia ({{continents.1.mean|int}} m), which holds the Tibetan Plateau, the Himalaya and the Pamirs. North America comes next ({{continents.2.mean|int}} m, lifted by Greenland's ice and the Rockies), then Africa ({{continents.3.mean|int}} m, a continent of high plateaus with few great peaks), South America ({{continents.4.mean|int}} m), Europe ({{continents.5.mean|int}} m) and Oceania ({{continents.6.mean|int}} m). Asia stands on average {{derived.asia_vs_europe|f1}} times as high as Europe.

A caution on small places. At {{grid.km_equator|f1}} km, a cell can hold a whole valley, so heights are smoothed: the highest cell in the grid, on the flank of Everest, is {{grid.highest_cell|int}} m, {{derived.everest_vs_cell|int}} m short of the summit. Countries smaller than about a hundred cells are only roughly resolved, and the ranking above includes only the {{countries.n_ranked|int}} countries that cover at least a hundred. Andorra, for instance, would rank among the highest of all, but it covers fewer than ten cells. The full table has every country, with small ones marked.

04 · MountainsThe peaks, checked against the ground

For the peaks I took every mountain, hill, summit and volcano in Wikidata with a height: {{peaks.n_wd|int}} items. Wikidata records each height's unit, so the {{peaks.ft_stated|int}} heights given in feet, most of them American and British, convert cleanly to metres. I still checked every height against the terrain around its point: a summit can rise well above the 10 km average around it, but not three times the local relief. That caught {{peaks.feet|int}} heights entered with the wrong unit and {{peaks.km|int}} given in kilometres, and dropped {{peaks.dropped|int}} items, most of them without coordinates or with a height that fitted nothing, leaving {{peaks.n|int}} summits on the map. Natural Earth and Wikipedia's list of the most prominent summits added only {{peaks.n_ne|int}} and {{peaks.n_wp|int}} summits that Wikidata lacked.

The highest peak of each continent

Metres above sea level. Continents as Natural Earth draws them, so Oceania's highest is Mount Wilhelm in Papua New Guinea; Puncak Jaya ({{peaks.prominent.8.elev|int}} m), the highest on New Guinea, falls in Indonesia and so in Asia.

Height is only one way to measure a mountain. Prominence asks how far you would have to descend from a summit before climbing anything higher, which picks out the great stand-alone mountains. Everest's prominence is its full height. Then come {{peaks.prominent.1.name|raw}} ({{peaks.prominent.1.prom|int}} m), {{peaks.prominent.2.name|raw}} ({{peaks.prominent.2.prom|int}} m) and {{peaks.prominent.3.name|raw}} ({{peaks.prominent.3.prom|int}} m), and the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta in Colombia, whose {{peaks.prominent.4.name|raw}} rises {{peaks.prominent.4.prom|int}} m from the Caribbean coast. K2, the second highest mountain, is only {{peaks.k2_prom_rank|ord}} by prominence, because it shares its base with the rest of the Karakoram.

05 · Where people liveHigh land, low cities

People do not live where the land is. Wikidata gives a height and a population for {{cities.n|int}} cities of 50,000 or more, holding {{cities.pop_bn|f1}} billion people between them. Half of those people live below {{cities.median_person|int}} m. While {{land.above.1000|pct}} of the world's land is above 1,000 m, only {{cities.share_above.1000|pct1}} of the cities' people live that high, and only {{cities.share_above.2000|pct1}} above 2,000 m, against {{land.above.2000|pct}} of the land: {{derived.land_above2000_vs_cities|f1}} times less, or {{derived.noant_above2000_vs_cities|f1}} times less leaving uninhabited Antarctica aside.

06 · Below sea levelThe world's sunken places

A little land lies below the sea's level, kept dry by deserts or dykes. On the {{grid.km_equator|f1}} km grid the named depressions add up to {{below.named|int}} km², and {{below.caspian_share_named|pct}} of that is the plain around the Caspian Sea, where {{cities.lowest.0.name|raw}}, {{cities.lowest.0.elev|abs}} m below sea level, is the lowest city of 100,000 or more in the data. Then come Egypt's Qattara Depression ({{below.g.qattara.area|int}} km²), the salt pan of Kati Thanda-Lake Eyre in Australia ({{below.g.eyre.area|int}} km²), the polders of the Netherlands ({{below.g.nl.area|int}} km²) and the Afar and Danakil depression in Ethiopia and Djibouti ({{below.g.afar.area|int}} km²). The Dead Sea, the lowest land on Earth, shows as a narrow trench reaching {{below.g.deadsea.min|abs}} m below sea level, and Death Valley as a handful of cells.

Land below sea level, by depression

These are approximate. A 10 km cell averages the depths of a depression with the ground around it, so small, deep places such as the Dead Sea's shore, Lake Assal in Djibouti or Death Valley's Badwater Basin look shallower and smaller than they are. The grid also finds another {{below.g.else.area|int}} km² of patches below 0 m on deltas and coastal flats, from the Po and the Danube to the Nile, which are partly real and partly the sea's depths bleeding into coastal cells; I leave them out of the total.

07 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

Terrain. The AWS Terrain Tiles are a global mosaic made by Mapzen in 2016 to 2017 and kept as an open dataset on Amazon. Each tile is a PNG whose colours encode height: height in metres = red × 256 + green + blue / 256 − 32,768. The detail differs by place: on land roughly 30 m (SRTM and national surveys) where available, coarser in polar regions and at sea. The page decodes them on the fly; colours, shading (light from the north-west, with vertical exaggeration that grows as you zoom out) and height steps are computed in your browser. Clicking reads the height from a detailed tile, at zoom 12 or finer, where a pixel is about 40 m across or less.

Statistics. All the shares and averages come from the {{grid.px|int}} × {{grid.px|int}} grid at zoom 4, about {{grid.km_equator|f1}} km at the equator and {{grid.km_60|f1}} km at 60°, in Web Mercator. Each cell is weighted by its true area on the globe. Land is Natural Earth's 1:10m countries less its lakes and reservoirs, rasterised at twice the grid's resolution so coastal cells count by their share of land. Coastal cells mix land and sea floor, so they are floored at 0 m; only wholly-land cells can be below sea level. Web Mercator stops at 85.05°, so Antarctica south of that line comes from ETOPO10, a 10 arc minute version of ETOPO1. {{grid.n_spikes|int}} corrupt cells (one claiming {{grid.spike_max|int}} m on Baffin Island) were replaced by the median of their neighbours. Ice counts at its surface.

Peaks. Wikidata mountains, hills, summits and volcanoes ({{peaks.n_rows|int}} rows, {{peaks.n_wd|int}} items), using the height Wikidata normalises to metres from each item's preferred statement, with {{peaks.n_ne|int}} summits added from Natural Earth's elevation points and {{peaks.n_wp|int}} from Wikipedia's list of peaks by prominence, which also gives the prominence ranking. Each height is tested against the highest and lowest 10 km cells within two cells of the point. Heights that only fit as feet or kilometres (a wrong unit in Wikidata) were converted; those that fit nothing were dropped, as were summits higher than the confirmed high point of the country they stand in. The independent list of national high points (from peakbagger.com, used only as a check and not republished) confirms {{peaks.hp_verified|int}} countries' high points.

Cities. Wikidata cities of 50,000 or more with a height and a population; duplicates merged, metropolitan areas, provinces and districts left out so people are not counted twice, and each height checked against the terrain in the same way. Wikidata's populations are of varying date and definition, and Chinese prefecture-level cities include large rural areas, which inflates China's share of high cities such as Zhaotong or Bijie; in the ranking of cities of a million or more, a Chinese city counts only if Natural Earth gives its urban area a million or more.

What this cannot say. The statistics are smoothed to about 10 km, so they understate peaks and narrow valleys and say little about tiny countries. Sea level itself is a reference surface, not a level the sea actually reaches everywhere; the tiles use mean sea level (the EGM96 geoid for SRTM). Mountain heights in Wikidata and Wikipedia come from many surveys of different ages, and Wikidata lists subsidiary tops (Everest's South Summit, for instance) as items of their own, so the map shows them alongside the main summits.
